Sources sit on a ladder of distance from the evidence. A **[primary source](/glossary/primary-source)** is the thing itself: the study, the law's text, the official documentation, the original dataset, the vendor's own spec sheet. A **secondary source** describes or summarizes a primary one — reporting, whitepapers, explainers. A **tertiary source** aggregates summaries — newsletters, listicles, and, notably, an AI answering from [memory](/glossary/training-cutoff). Every hop up the ladder loses a caveat, rounds a number or adds an interpretation.

## How meaning bends on the way up

A study abstract might say: "a pilot with 40 participants measured up to a 12% improvement on one task measure". One level up: "teams got 12% better". Another level up: "'big' performance gains found". Same research, three different promises — and only the first one is what the researchers claim.

## When secondary is fine

Orientation. Deciding what matters, what the vocabulary is, where to look. Secondary sources are how you find the primary ones. Being maximally skeptical of everything is not the skill; directing skepticism is.

## When you must trace

- Numbers you will repeat — in a deck, a report, a purchase decision.
- Anything legal, medical or financial.
- Claims that start with "studies show" or "research suggests".
- Anything a stranger's money depends on.

## How to trace a claim in practice

1. Get a pointer: title, authors, year, publisher — ask for the full citation if it is missing.
2. Search the exact title; open the abstract or first pages.
3. Find the number and read the sentence around it: what does the original actually claim?
4. If every chain you follow ends in "an article about a report about a study", the claim is not research — it is a telephone game. Say so.

## A bad example

> **Statement for your board memo:** "The pitch deck says teams using AI assistants ship 40% faster."
>
> Deck → whitepaper → study → survey. Each hop chose its favorite number. Shipping "40% faster" under your name inherits all four hops' optimism.

## A better example

> **After tracing:** "In one 2024 study of 2,400 teams, routine-task completion improved 40% during the pilot; the authors note the effect may not generalize."
>
> Same number, honest boundaries, and you can name the document it lives in. That sentence survives a skeptical reader.

## Transfer

- Negotiations: the other side's "industry data" gets traced before it constrains your offer.
- Policy and compliance: read the rule, not the summary of the rule.
- Recommendations: "according to research" is not a source — it is an invitation to go find one.

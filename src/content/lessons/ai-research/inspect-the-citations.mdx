A citation is a claim about a source — and like any claim it can be wrong. Inspecting citations means opening the source and confirming two things: **it exists**, and **it says what the answer says it says**. Three failure patterns cover most of what you will find.

## The three failures

1. **The source does not exist.** Plausible study titles, researcher names and page names, generated to look real — [hallucinated](/glossary/hallucination) from top to bottom. If a search for the exact title finds nothing at all, stop there — an unfindable source is a non-source.
2. **The source exists but does not say that.** The number is off by a factor, the quote is trimmed or invented, the study says "may be associated with" and the answer says "proven". Search the exact phrase in quotes and compare wording.
3. **The source says it, but the source is weak.** It exists and matches — and it is a blog post about a survey about a pilot. Real, but far from the [primary source](/glossary/primary-source); sorting quality is [the next lesson](/learn/ai-research/primary-vs-secondary-sources).

## Checking in one minute

- Search the **exact title or quote** in quotation marks. Zero results → treat the claim as invented until proven otherwise.
- Open the source and find the number or sentence — browser search on the page is enough.
- For quotes, check the exact words **and** who said them.
- For numbers, check the number and the direction. "13% more productive" is not the same as "a 13% productivity difference", and neither is the same as "13% of the effect".
- One honest caveat: a working link is not proof either. Links can be real while the claim misreads them — the check is the text, not the link.

## A bad example

> **Answer:** "A 2021 Stanford study found remote workers are 13% more productive."
>
> No title, no authors, no link — you cannot even begin to check it. Treat the number as unverified; a precise statistic riding a vague citation is exactly where misattribution hides.

## A better example

> **Ask:** "Give the full citation for that study — title, authors, year — plus the exact sentence behind the 13% figure, and a link if you have one."
>
> Now you have a searchable pointer and a sentence to match. One search, one page-search, done.

## The habit

When output will be reused — published, forwarded, put in a deck — the citations are your first stop, not the prose. Quote-first answers make this cheap: the quote is the claim, and the check is comparing it with the source. That is the same move as [grounding](/learn/prompting/ground-responses-in-your-sources), pointed at someone else's sources instead of yours.

## Transfer

- Articles and briefs: no specific claim ships without a findable source behind it.
- Health, law and money: every citation gets opened, especially when the answer reads beautifully.
- Someone else's AI-assisted draft: run the three failure patterns over it before you co-sign.

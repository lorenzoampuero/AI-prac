Framing is what the viewer sees **first** — and in image prompts it is four controllable decisions: **shot scale** (how much of the subject fills the frame), **placement** (where it sits), **negative space** (how calm the rest is) and **[aspect ratio](/glossary/aspect-ratio)** (the shape of the canvas). Say nothing, and the model picks all four for you. Often badly.

## Shot scale

- **Close-up** — texture, expression, intimacy. Great for products and faces.
- **Medium** — subject plus a hint of context. The default for editorial images.
- **Wide** — environment is the story; the subject anchors it.

## Placement and negative space

Name where things sit: “subject on the left third”, “centered, symmetrical”, “low in frame with sky above”. Negative space is a tool, not waste — a calm right side is where a headline will go later. Designers ask for it on purpose: “wide calm space on the right, nothing in it”.

## Aspect ratio comes first

Choose the canvas before composing: **1:1** for avatars and grids, **16:9** for headers and slides, **9:16** for stories and posters. A composition built for a square rarely survives a crop to widescreen — deciding the ratio first means the framing is designed for it.

## A bad example

> **Ask:** “A busy scene with everything important in the center.”
>
> Dense center-compositions leave no room for text, crop badly, and give the eye nowhere to rest.

## A better example

> **Ask:** “Medium shot of a potter’s hands shaping a bowl, centered, shelves softly blurred behind, generous negative space above, 4:5 portrait.”
>
> Scale, placement, space and canvas — each stated, each fixable in the next iteration.

## Why it works

Framing decisions are independent dials: change the shot scale without touching the light, change the ratio without re-describing the scene. Models follow these words well enough to be useful, and when a composition disappoints, you know exactly which dial to turn.

## Transfer

- Slides: reserve negative space where titles will actually sit.
- Social formats: pick 1:1 / 16:9 / 9:16 first, then compose for it.
- Iteration: if the frame feels wrong, fix placement and scale before touching style.

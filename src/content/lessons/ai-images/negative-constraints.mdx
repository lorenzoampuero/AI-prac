“No umbrellas” is a wish, and wishes are weak. A model renders what you describe — and naming a thing, even to forbid it, puts that thing in play. Negative constraints work when you **convert them**: describe the wanted state where the unwanted element cannot exist, or remove it afterwards with an edit.

## Why wishes are weak

Every word you write is something the model can draw. “A beach with no elephants” mentions elephants; the words carry weight in the same space as everything else. Rendered results show it: forbidden objects drift back in, watermarks shade into “no watermark” prompts, and “don’t make it dark” produces very dark images. The wish states what you fear; the model renders what is stated.

## Convert instead of forbid

Describe the state of the world where the unwanted thing does not exist:

- “no people” → “a deserted street at dawn”
- “no text” → “plain unmarked packaging, bare surfaces”
- “not blurry” → “sharp focus throughout”
- “no clutter” → “minimal composition with generous empty space”
- “don’t make it dark” → “bright, evenly lit”

Each conversion is renderable, checkable, and leaves nothing for the model to mis-draw.

## What negative fields can and cannot do

Some tools — often those you run or fine-tune yourself — offer an explicit **negative prompt** field: a list of things to steer away from. Where offered, it behaves as a nudge rather than a law: keep it short and concrete (a handful of nouns, not paragraphs of wishes), and treat the output as *probably* clear, not provably clear. Many hosted tools have no such parameter at all; there, conversion is the whole toolkit. Either way, the reliable mechanism for true removal comes next.

## Removal by editing

Where a tool supports edits, unwanted elements leave through the region: you supply the image, mark the area to change — a mask — and describe its replacement. Current editing APIs work this way: OpenAI's image edits endpoint accepts “a mask that identifies the areas to replace”, and the tool's own documentation notes that masking is entirely prompt-based — the model uses the mask as guidance but may not follow its exact shape with complete precision. So the professional loop for “make that thing gone” is: generate the scene close to right, then edit the region with a clear description of what should be there instead, then inspect that area closely. This is the same inpaint manoeuvre from the [edit loop](/learn/ai-images/iterate-and-edit), aimed at subtraction instead of adjustment.

## A bad example

> **Ask:** “Office photo, no clutter, no people, don’t make it dark, no logos.”
>
> Four wishes and one instruction about the thing being described. The result has a good chance of a person-shaped blur, a dark corner, and a logo-like smudge.

## A better example

> **Ask:** “A tidy sunlit office, empty desk with a closed laptop, bright even light, plain walls.”
>
> Everything is a state the model can render — and the unwanted elements have nowhere to appear.

## Why it works

Both halves are the track so far, pointed at removal. Conversion keeps you in the mode models handle best: rendering described states, not interpreting prohibitions. Editing handles what conversion misses: actual pixels get replaced in the actual region. Wishes do neither — they describe the absence of the preferred arrangement until it returns under another name.

## Transfer

- Briefs in any tool: scan for “no / not / don’t” and convert each into the wanted state.
- Cleanliness in product shots: “bare, spotless, empty” beats “no mess” every time.
- True removal: mask the region and describe the replacement, then inspect closely.
- Anywhere you need certainty: the deliverable gets checked — wishes never shipped anything.
